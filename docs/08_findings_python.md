# Findings — Python Analysis
## Marketplace Analytics Platform

This memo summarizes the findings from the four notebooks (`05_customer_analysis`, `06_seller_analysis`, `07_operations_analysis`, `08_product_analysis`), which build on the descriptive SQL findings in `docs/07_findings_sql.md` with regression, hypothesis testing, and clustering. Where a SQL finding is referenced, it reflects the prior phase's work; every number below comes from real data — no assumed costs or synthetic figures were introduced anywhere in this phase.

## Customer Analysis

The retention crisis SQL identified (0.45% weighted period-1 cohort retention) holds up at the customer-lifetime level too: only 3.00% of all 93,358 customers (2,801 people) ever place a second order. The cohort heatmap shows this drop-off is uniform across every cohort from September 2016 through August 2018 — every cohort falls under 1% retention by period 1, with no month showing materially stronger repeat behavior — ruling out a seasonal or early-business-era explanation. This is a structural feature of the platform, not a phase it will grow out of.

First-purchase category does meaningfully predict who comes back. Customers whose first order was in `home_appliances`, `fashion_male_clothing`, or `fashion_bags_accessories` return at 5.6–9.0%, roughly seven to sixteen times the rate of `computers` or `books_technical` buyers (0.6–0.8%). The product notebook's deeper cross-tabulation by state and payment method confirms this is a genuine category effect rather than a geographic or payment-method artifact for the categories tested there — though it's a directional tendency, not an absolute ranking that holds in every single state/payment cut.

The top 1% of customers by projected 12-month CLV (934 people) spend 15.2 times the average customer (BRL 2,988.63 vs. BRL 196.49). They don't cluster by state — the distribution roughly tracks the overall base, with SP slightly under-indexed (37.9% vs. 41.9%) — but they do cluster by category: `musical_instruments` buyers are about 5.2 times over-represented (3.1% vs. 0.6%) and `furniture_decor` buyers about 1.3 times over-represented (8.4% vs. 6.3%), with a mild skew toward credit card (77.3% vs. 75.5%). Notably, `health_beauty`, the platform's single largest revenue category, is *under*-represented among top-value customers (5.9% vs. 9.0%), meaning the category driving the most revenue and the category producing the most valuable customers are not the same thing.

Among the small pool of repeat customers, staying active longer does correlate with more total spend, and the effect is statistically real (p=0.027) — but it explains almost none of the variation (R²=0.0017). A customer who stays active an extra month is worth roughly BRL 3.44 more on average. This is too small an effect to build a retention strategy around in isolation.

Finally, RFM segmentation is a reliable stand-in for CLV tier at the extremes — `Champions`, `Cannot Lose Them`, and `Loyal` land 78–99% in the High CLV tier (avg CLV BRL 365–474), while `Potential Loyal`, `About to Sleep`, `Promising`, and `Recent` land 79–99% in Low (avg CLV BRL 40–60) — but it breaks down for `At Risk` and `Need Attention`. Despite the alarming names, 86%+ of both segments actually fall in the **Mid** CLV tier, with average CLV (~BRL 112) well below `Loyal` (BRL 365). These are average customers who've gone quiet, not high-value customers about to be lost. Retention spend prioritized by RFM label alone risks over-investing here.

## Seller Analysis

K-means clustering on review score, on-time rate, revenue, and cancellation rate found the data naturally supports only **two** seller groups, not the four tiers (`Elite`/`Strong`/`Average`/`Needs Work`) SQL's manual composite score implies — silhouette score peaks sharply at k=2 (0.41) and never recovers past 0.31 at any other k. `Elite` sellers map almost perfectly onto the stronger cluster (98.1%), but most `Strong`-tier sellers (60.7%) actually behave statistically like the weaker cluster. The real dividing line in the data is Elite vs. everyone else.

This has a direct financial consequence. By the manual tier, revenue at risk from quality-troubled sellers looks tiny — `Average` and `Needs Work` combined are 16 sellers generating just 0.22% of platform revenue. But the cluster-based cross-check tells a different story: the statistically low-quality group accounts for 6.20% of scored-seller revenue (BRL 920,600.16) — roughly 28 times more exposure than the manual tier suggests. The gap exists because SQL's composite score weights revenue percentile at 20%, letting a seller with real quality problems buy their way into `Strong` if their sales volume is high enough — `Strong` sellers alone (BRL 689,560.32, 4.47% of total revenue) are 60.7% Cluster 1 per the crosstab above.

Individual outlier detection found no seller combining very high revenue with very poor quality at a strict threshold — the correlation between seller revenue and quality is essentially zero (0.0086), meaning sellers with real quality problems generally don't scale to high volume in the first place. One seller stood out at a relaxed threshold with a genuinely alarming profile (quality z-score of -4.15, 35.71% on-time rate, 11.11% cancellation) and is worth individual account-management follow-up.

The correlation matrix reinforces this: review score, on-time rate, and cancellation rate all correlate with each other as expected (review vs. on-time r=0.46; review vs. cancellation r=-0.26; on-time vs. cancellation r=-0.08, all p<0.001), but none of them correlate meaningfully with revenue (all |r|≤0.05). Quality and scale are statistically independent dimensions on this platform — which is exactly why a composite score blending them produces a misleading tier.

Seller revenue concentration is extreme by any standard: Gini coefficient of 0.79–0.80, with the top 19% of active sellers generating 80.51% of revenue — a near-exact match to SQL's existing Pareto finding, now with formal statistical backing.

## Operations Analysis

Delivery time is the single strongest, most practically significant relationship found anywhere in Python Analysis. Each additional day of delivery costs 0.0453 review-score points (95% CI does not cross zero, p≈0), and this relationship has real explanatory power — R²=0.1113, far above any other regression run in this project. Scaled up, this matches SQL's descriptive 1.72-point gap between on-time (4.29) and late (2.57) orders almost exactly.

That said, the revenue consequence of late delivery via repeat purchases specifically is small. Customers whose *first* order arrived late do return at a significantly lower rate (2.51% vs. 3.04%, p=0.01), but because repeat purchasing is already so rare platform-wide, the estimated revenue impact is only about BRL 6,466 — negligible against total platform revenue. Fixing delivery speed is worth doing for review score and reputation, but it isn't a meaningful revenue lever through this specific channel.

Payment method genuinely affects order value, not just because of category mix: credit card orders are significantly larger than boleto (median BRL 109.10 vs. BRL 93.66 overall), and this holds within 9 of the top 10 categories after controlling for category — likely reflecting installment flexibility rather than which categories credit-card shoppers happen to favor. `computers_accessories` is the one clean exception, showing no significant difference. Installment depth itself correlates positively with order value (Spearman rho=0.46 within credit card orders), consistent with SQL's descriptive 129→343 BRL finding, though the direction of causation (bigger baskets choosing more installments, vs. installments enabling bigger baskets) can't be disentangled from this data alone.

Regional delivery inequality is stark and statistically overwhelming (Kruskal-Wallis across five macro-regions, p≈0). North Brazil customers wait a median 20 days for delivery versus 12 for the South — roughly 64% longer — reinforcing the seller-supply gaps SQL already flagged in states like PA and MA. One nuance: the Northeast has a *worse* on-time rate (87.50%) than the North (91.71%) despite shorter absolute delivery times, suggesting the North's delivery estimates are set more realistically for its known logistics challenges, while the Northeast's estimates are comparatively optimistic relative to what's actually delivered.

## Product Analysis

A Random Forest model predicting review score from delivery days, freight percentage, category, state, and payment type together confirms delivery time isn't just the strongest single factor — it's nearly the *only* factor that matters. Delivery days account for 81% of feature importance, with state and freight percentage each contributing 7%, category 4%, and payment type 1%. The full model's test R² (0.1491) is barely higher than delivery days alone (0.1113), meaning the other four inputs add very little once delivery time is already known. This isn't really a multivariate problem — it's a delivery-time problem with a long tail of minor contributors.

This directly reframes SQL's `office_furniture` finding. Freight cost, tested on its own, is statistically significant (p≈0) but practically negligible for review score (R²=0.0007) — moving from 10% to 20% freight share predicts only a 0.03-point drop. `office_furniture`'s poor 3.64 review score is not well explained by its 20% freight burden; its 20.4-day delivery time is almost certainly the real driver. Notably, category clustering (below) places `office_furniture` in the platform's high-revenue, decently-reviewed core group rather than a low-quality cluster, reinforcing that its problem is specific to delivery time rather than a trait it shares with a broader set of troubled categories.

The category-level repeat-purchase pattern from the customer analysis largely holds up under closer scrutiny, though not as an absolute rule. Cross-tabulated by both payment method and customer state, `health_beauty` is consistently among the lowest-repeat categories — though not the strict lowest in every cut, since `computers_accessories` and `sports_leisure` dip below it in several states and one payment-type cell — while `furniture_decor` and `bed_bath_table` remain consistently higher across essentially every cut tested. This is a genuine category effect, not a payment or geography artifact, but it's best read as a directional tendency (aside from expected noise in small debit-card/voucher cells, where sample sizes drop to double digits) rather than a category that dominates every single comparison.

A natural-experiment test of review response speed — comparing 1-star reviewers who got a response within 24 hours against those who waited longer — found no significant relationship with repeat purchase (p=0.3785); if anything, the fast-response group had a slightly *lower* repeat rate (5.76% vs. 6.24%). There is no data-backed case for prioritizing 1-star response speed as a revenue lever: by the time any response arrives (median 38 hours), the bad experience that generated the review has already happened and likely already determined whether the customer returns. Faster response may still be worth doing for reputational reasons, but not on this specific financial basis.

Clustering all 74 categories on revenue, review score, freight percentage, and repeat rate (k=4, chosen by silhouette score of 0.29) produced four groups that split revenue, reviews, and loyalty into almost entirely different sets: a 33-category revenue core (`health_beauty`, `bed_bath_table`, `sports_leisure`, `office_furniture`, etc. — BRL 432,437.50 avg revenue, solid 4.12 avg review score, but a low 0.96% repeat rate); a 34-category small-revenue, well-reviewed cluster (`electronics`, `furniture_living_room`, etc. — BRL 30,725.03 avg revenue, the best review score of any cluster at 4.25, highest freight share at 18.43%, low 0.62% repeat rate); a 4-category small-but-sticky cluster (`home_appliances`, `arts_and_craftmanship`, `diapers_and_hygiene`, `home_comfort_2` — BRL 24,675.67 avg revenue, 4.04 avg review, and by far the best repeat rate of any cluster at 6.33%, roughly six to ten times every other cluster); and a genuinely troubled 3-category cluster (`portable_kitchen_food_preparators`, `gaming_pc`, `security_and_services` — lowest revenue at BRL 1,994.22, lowest review score at 3.18, and zero repeat purchases). The categories that drive revenue, the categories with the best reviews, and the categories that build loyalty are three almost entirely different sets — and critically, `office_furniture` sits in the healthy revenue-core cluster, not the troubled one, reinforcing that its problem is an isolated delivery-time issue rather than a shared cluster-level weakness.

## Cross-Notebook Synthesis

Three themes run through all four notebooks. First, the retention problem is real, structural, and not explained away by any single factor tested — not customer lifespan, not delivery speed's knock-on effect on repeat purchases, not review response time. It appears to be a fundamental characteristic of how this marketplace operates, not a fixable operational gap.

Second, delivery time is by a wide margin the most powerful, statistically robust lever found in this entire phase — for review score directly, dominating even a multivariate model built specifically to find competing explanations. Several other intuitive levers that seemed plausible going in — freight cost, review response speed, customer lifespan — turned out to be statistically real but practically negligible once tested rigorously. This is a meaningful distinction the analysis is careful to preserve throughout: statistical significance is not the same as a lever worth pulling.

Third, manual and composite scoring systems built earlier in this project (seller `performance_tier`, RFM segments) both have specific, identifiable blind spots that only became visible once tested against the underlying data directly: the seller score understates revenue-at-risk by roughly 28x because it partly rewards the same revenue that's masking a quality problem, and two RFM segments (`At Risk`, `Need Attention`) are mislabeled as high-value when the data says they're merely average.

## Recommendations

- Prioritize delivery speed as the highest-confidence lever for review score improvement — it's the strongest, most consistent finding across every notebook in this phase.
- Use CLV tier and the seller K-means cluster, not RFM segment names or the manual `performance_tier`, to prioritize retention and seller-intervention spend — both manual systems mislabel a specific segment in ways that would misallocate effort.
- Weight acquisition spend toward categories with structurally higher repeat rates (`home_appliances`, `fashion_male_clothing`, `fashion_bags_accessories`, `bed_bath_table`, `furniture_decor`) rather than treating all first purchases as equally likely to build a returning customer.
- Do not invest in 1-star review response speed as a repeat-purchase revenue play; if pursued, justify it on reputational or brand-perception grounds instead, since no revenue case exists in this data.
- Treat `office_furniture` as an isolated delivery-time problem, not a freight-cost issue and not a shared cluster-level trait — category clustering places it firmly in the healthy revenue core, not a troubled group.
- Prioritize the North/Northeast delivery gap directly — it's the starkest, most statistically overwhelming operational inequality found in this analysis.

## Limitations

CLV figures throughout rely on a simplified purchase-rate projection (not BG/NBD) and should be read as directional rather than precise forecasts. Several analyses necessarily worked from smaller sub-populations — repeat buyers are only 3% of customers, scored sellers require a 5-order minimum, and top-1%-CLV or outlier-seller groups are small by definition — so within-group comparisons carry more noise than platform-wide figures. Category attribution (a customer or order's "category") uses the highest-value line item where multiple categories appear in one order, which loses some nuance for multi-item orders. No customer acquisition cost data exists in the source dataset, so a payback-period analysis was deliberately not modeled rather than built on an assumed figure, consistent with this project's real-data-only principle. Finally, several statistically significant findings (freight cost, customer lifespan, review response speed) have near-zero practical effect sizes and are explicitly flagged as such throughout rather than conflated with delivery time's genuinely large effect.
